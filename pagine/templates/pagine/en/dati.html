The data is the European air quality forecast of the Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service (CAMS), the service of the European Union's Copernicus programme operated by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF). It is open and free data, downloaded from the Atmosphere Data Store.

Which forecast we use

  • "Ensemble" model: not a single model, but the combination (median) of the forecasts of about ten European models. It is the most robust version CAMS provides.
  • Resolution: one cell every 0.1 degrees, i.e. about 11 km north-south and 7–9 km east-west at Italian latitudes. Each cell has a single value: differences within the cell (a street, a park) are not visible.
  • Area covered: all of Europe, from North Africa to Scandinavia.
  • Level: values refer to the air near the ground, the air we breathe.
  • Unit: micrograms per cubic metre (µg/m³), hourly values.

Pollutants and thresholds

For each pollutant the map compares the value with a reference threshold: below the threshold colours go from cyan to green, at the threshold they turn yellow, above it they move to red and, above twice the threshold, to purple.

The thresholds are the limits of European legislation in force from 2030 (Directive 2024/2881): the daily limits for particulate matter, nitrogen dioxide and sulphur dioxide, and the target value for ozone (average over 8 hours). They are the strictest European references, chosen on purpose: the hourly limits for NO₂ and SO₂ only flag acute episodes, while this site wants to make everyday exposure visible. The limits apply to the average of a whole day (or of 8 hours), while the map shows the value of a single hour: the comparison is indicative, not a check of compliance with legal limits.

Why the ozone map is coloured almost everywhere. Ozone is not emitted directly: it forms in the air from other pollutants under sunlight and spreads over large areas, also far from cities. Across Europe there is a widespread background of roughly 60–100 µg/m³, so the ozone map often shows green to yellow over the whole continent, without the clean areas and local hotspots seen for the other pollutants. It is not an error: it is the real picture, and in summer, with stronger sunlight, values above the threshold become common in many areas.
